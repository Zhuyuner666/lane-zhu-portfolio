Kong 空

Rehearsal notebook

Lane Zhu

Kong

Performer and co-creator

Set in an ancient Jianghu tavern, the piece follows characters who compete for a secret manual.

Our chosen starting point for this piece was the Chinese character KONG (empty), which we associate with the cultural symbol of Chinese, Kung fu, and think of the line from The Diamond Sutra. “All phenomena are illusory”. Extending from them, we want to explore humans’ tendency to pursue the meaning of life so intensely that they forget we are born with nothing with the cultural background of the swordsman.

The two goals of our production are comedy and magical realism. We’ll use a linear, narrative script to make sure the plot is logical, realistic, and easy to follow. The acting should be exaggerated and comedic. The lighting, setting, and props are all designed for magical realism. Acrobatic fighting is a significant part of supporting magical realism as well.

Our target audience is the public, especially those who are struggling to achieve the value of meaning. By witnessing each character chase it with absolute conviction, the audience should be shocked. The audience's emotions should fluctuate along with the plot. They are invited to question why we seek meaning at all—and realize that the search itself may be the only meaning we ever hold.

From Kong to Jianghu

At the beginning of creating this piece, we first discussed what kind of theatre we wanted to present. In our vision, this piece had to connect with contemporary society and stimulate audience reflection. At the same time, we also wanted to prevent the piece from becoming too rigid and serious, because we wanted the audience to gain a certain degree of entertainment. We proposed themes such as animal protection, but during the workshops, the members mainly presented these ideas by crawling and imitating animals. After several attempts, we found that these movements did not create clear character relationships or conflicts that could be developed further. Therefore, the group decided to abandon this direction.

The real breakthrough came from a group discussion. When we once again complained that we “could not find a sufficiently meaningful theme,” one member suggested whether the group’s constant search for meaning, while never being able to determine that meaning, could itself become the starting point of the piece. This suggestion changed the direction of the discussion.

Instead of continuing to search for new social issues, we wrote down many associated words on paper around “Kong,” meaning “empty.” During this process, “Kong” made us think of “kung fu,” and the group members unanimously agreed that this was very interesting. From this, we began a physical workshop focused on exploring combat.

Combat and Character Development

During that workshop, one member casually picked up a bamboo prop used in theatre class, treated it as a weapon, and began chasing the other members. The other members responded by dodging, blocking, and chasing. These movements immediately required another performer to respond, naturally creating competition, changes in status, and comedic rhythm between the characters. The bamboo prop also made us think of a bamboo forest, which has the characteristics of Chinese wuxia films. From this, the group discovered that the Chinese Jianghu world could become a way of transforming the idea of “searching” into stage action.

Exploring combat with bamboo props.

Initially, The Girl’s mother was designed as a symbol of The Girl’s inner strength. She stood on a raised platform behind The Girl and imitated her movements. Through this, we hoped to suggest that she was providing spiritual support to her daughter during the fight. However, this symbolic staging was not effectively communicated to the audience. Feedback data from the first presentation showed that only 2 out of 14 people fully understood the mother’s role. Therefore, we removed the role of the mother.

The early staging of The Girl’s mother.

We also redeveloped one character from The Girl’s mother into the Tavern Owner after discovering that the performer could play the pipa. The tavern allowed the characters to share the same space before chasing the secret manual, observe one another in advance, and establish smaller conflicts. In later rehearsals, the group had the characters enter the tavern with different purposes and tested how they could observe, probe, and interrupt one another before formally competing for the secret manual.

Performing the Tavern Owner

I sit on the black block at centre stage and use the pipa to establish the atmosphere of ancient China. I deliberately allow the music to enter before any dialogue so that the audience is first drawn into the scene through the sound of the instrument and my physical posture, creating a mysterious ancient atmosphere.

I choose to play Ambush from Ten Sides, whose rhythm suggests warfare and hidden danger. This implies that the tavern is not a safe space but a place like a battlefield, hinting that every character may have hidden intentions. I build this tension through the musical progression as the strumming gradually grows from soft to stronger and then softens again before transitioning into a rapid five-finger tremolo, which intensifies the rhythm and creates the feeling that danger is approaching.

When Fool attempts to sell books to me, I lower the corners of my mouth and briefly glance over her without maintaining eye contact. I stand up and move away from her, then strike her with the pipa while shouting “Get out” in a rude and loud voice. This shows that the Tavern Owner has the power to decide who can remain in the tavern, while placing Fool in the position of being driven away and disrespected.

When White’s voice enters, I immediately change my body and voice. I actively approach her, place my hand on her shoulder, and hold her arm before smiling brightly and bringing her wine. The reduced physical distance, active touch, and gentle tone collectively show the Tavern Owner’s deliberate attempt to please White and also suggest that White possesses a higher social status.

When Black enters and asks for wine, my head and gaze remain directed towards White, and I maintain an almost obsessive smile while casually placing a bowl of wine on the table. My head and gaze shift rapidly between them, allowing the audience to repeatedly see this favouritism. This clear and exaggerated difference in treatment has a comedic effect. However, there is also discomfort beneath the comedy: my attitude shows that the characters’ value is not determined by their behaviour but by the Tavern Owner’s judgement of their identities and interests.

Creating and Directing Comedy

I proposed the first interaction between Fool and The Girl as a set of physical movements repeated three times. While Fool organises the fake secret manuals in his hands and attempts to hide the letter left by The Girl’s mother, The Girl approaches him from behind three consecutive times and secretly looks at the fake secret manual. Each time Fool turns around to check, The Girl quickly turns her head in the same direction as him, making Fool believe that he has not been discovered. During the final repetition, the sudden eye contact between them breaks the expectation and creates a comedic ending.

The audience knows that The Girl is secretly looking, while Fool still believes that he controls the situation. This difference in information creates dramatic irony, making his self-satisfied cleverness appear even more ridiculous.

After The Girl agrees to search for the secret manual, I make the same music appear again and create a second set of movements repeated three times. However, this time, their positions and power relationship are reversed, as Fool actively leads and controls her. Fool walks in front and points in different directions. Wherever his finger points, The Girl immediately crouches down and searches in the corresponding location. These two sets of movements, which echo one another, show that The Girl has changed into a follower controlled by Fool.

I also created Fool’s line: “It has the truth of your mother’s death.” Fool uses this line to transform The Girl’s emotions towards her mother into her motivation for searching for the secret manual. Her repeated movements of continuously crouching down to search while finding nothing transform the character’s futile but persistent pursuit into a clear stage image.

Directing Fool and The Girl

I wanted Fool to display both cunning and comical characteristics. When The Girl drags Fool while moving, I directed Fool to break the fourth wall, point at The Girl, make an exaggerated expression towards the audience, pretend to complain, and produce screams that clearly exceeded the actual degree of pain. Afterwards, when he deceives The Girl into searching for her mother’s letter near the counter, I directed him to raise the real letter and show it to the audience while her back is turned towards him, combining this with raised eyebrows and a shushing gesture.

I also directed The Girl to use a deliberately clumsy physical state to show her innocence. After failing to snatch the letter from Fool, she needs to take several consecutive steps backwards before regaining her balance. When Fool has already walked several steps forward, she remains standing in the same place and looks from side to side as if she has completely failed to notice where he is, only hurriedly following him after Fool scolds her. The Girl’s slowness and loss of balance strengthen the comedic effect. However, the audience can also realise that she is willing to believe the meaning Fool has fabricated for the secret manual because she desperately wants to understand her mother’s death. Her comedic movements are connected with people’s obsession with meaning, allowing the audience, after laughing, to recognise that they may similarly become trapped in a blind search because of their desire for answers.

Staging

Because the school’s black box stage initially appeared visually monotonous, we began thinking about what elements could be added to create a wuxia atmosphere. First, we tried placing several black boxes on the ground to simulate rocks and create a mysterious mountain environment. However, we found that the boxes were difficult to move and affected the characters’ movements. We then tried attaching bamboo to the black boxes with black tape to simulate a forest, but the final effect was unattractive and bulky.

We continued researching and were inspired by the white gauze used in an online ancient Chinese-style dance video, so we purchased white gauze. During the first test, the lighting fixture could be seen by the audience, and the lighting did not create sufficient spatial depth. We moved the light behind the white gauze, used a black box to conceal the light source, and tested the lighting from different angles while asking other members to stand in the audience’s position and observe the effect.

We unexpectedly discovered that when the light shone from a diagonal angle, the gauze could catch the light, while the black box blocked part of it, eventually producing a shadow onstage that resembled an open doorframe. This effect had not appeared in our original design drawing. We decided to keep it because it allowed the same piece of gauze to function both as a spatial boundary and as an illuminated stage background.

Light & Space

We used the gauze as the sacred place where the secret manual was stored and dimmed the lighting after Black took away the secret manual, creating the effect that the secret manual carrying everyone’s expectations had been taken away.

In the set design, I used the red Chinese couplets on the wall behind the characters and the black box representing a counter at the front of the stage to establish the tavern environment. When Fool and The Girl began searching for the secret manual, I designed them to gradually tear the couplets from the wall as they moved. After they left the stage, the counter was also removed during the blackout, suggesting that the characters had left their original stable space and begun a longer search.
